Take a moment to study Málaga Cathedral from the square. Its broad façade, tall tower and mixture of architectural styles tell a story that stretches across several generations.

After Christian forces captured Málaga in 1487, the city’s main mosque was adapted for Christian worship. A cathedral chapter was established, and the present cathedral gradually replaced the earlier arrangements. Construction began in the sixteenth century, but work continued for so long that the building reflects changing tastes and ambitions. Renaissance ideas shaped much of the design, while later work introduced more ornate Baroque features.

Look at the main façade and then compare the two sides of the upper outline. One tower rises prominently; the other was never completed. This is why locals call the cathedral La Manquita, usually translated as “the one-armed lady”. The nickname is affectionate, but it also points to a real gap between the original ambitions and the finished building. Popular explanations often blame a single diversion of funds, yet the construction history involved a much longer series of decisions, interruptions and financial constraints. It is safer to see the missing tower as the result of an unfinished project than to repeat one neat legend as settled fact.

The building’s scale was deliberate. A cathedral was not only a place for worship: it was also a public statement about authority, identity and the place of Málaga within Christian Spain. Its position in the historic centre connected religious life with the city’s streets, markets and civic spaces.

If you enter, allow your eyes time to adjust to the interior’s height and the rhythm of its columns and chapels. The decoration was assembled over centuries, so the cathedral is not a single frozen moment in architectural history. It is a record of work begun by one generation and continued, altered or left unfinished by others.

From the square, the missing tower is easy to treat as a flaw. Instead, it offers a useful way to read the building: Málaga Cathedral is both a monument to ambition and a reminder that history rarely follows the original plan.
