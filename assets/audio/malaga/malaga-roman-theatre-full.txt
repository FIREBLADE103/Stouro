Stand where you can see the curve of the seating and the relationship between the theatre and the Alcazaba above it. Málaga’s Roman Theatre was built in the early first century CE, during the reign of Augustus, when Roman cities across the empire were developing public buildings that expressed civic life and Roman identity.

A theatre was more than a place to watch a performance. It brought people into a shared public space, organised them by seating and status, and gave the city a setting for drama, music and public occasions. The surviving seating follows the slope of the hillside, an economical building technique used in many ancient theatres. The curved rows focused attention toward the performance area, while entrances and passages helped spectators reach their places.

The theatre was used for roughly two centuries, although activity declined before it was eventually abandoned as a performance venue. Later, the site became part of the changing urban landscape around the hill. Stone and architectural pieces from Roman buildings were reused in later construction, including the Alcazaba. This was not unusual in the ancient and medieval world: prepared stone was expensive to quarry and shape, so older buildings often supplied materials for new ones. The reuse also means that Roman fragments can survive in a different setting from the one for which they were originally made.

The theatre remained buried beneath later development for many centuries. It was identified in 1951 during work in the area, and subsequent archaeological investigation revealed more of its structure. Further excavation and restoration made the remains accessible to the public. The discovery is a reminder that a city’s history is not always visible at street level; sometimes it survives beneath later buildings until chance and careful research bring it back into view.

Look at the theatre in relation to the Alcazaba. The two monuments belong to different political and cultural periods, but they occupy the same strategic hillside. The Roman theatre reflects a city organised around Roman public life. The fortress above it developed during the Islamic period and later became part of the city’s defensive system. Later builders reused some Roman material, linking the structures physically as well as geographically.

What you see today is only part of the original theatre, and the modern setting differs greatly from the ancient one. Even so, the surviving curve of the seating lets you imagine the basic experience: people gathering, finding their place and turning their attention toward a performance. The theatre connects Málaga to the wider Roman world while also showing how later generations transformed the remains of that world.
