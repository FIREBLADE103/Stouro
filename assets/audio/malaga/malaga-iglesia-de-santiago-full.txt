Iglesia de Santiago is one of Málaga’s oldest Christian churches and a useful place to see how the city’s architecture changed after the Christian conquest of 1487. Its history is not limited to a single building campaign. The church developed over time, and its appearance preserves traces of different periods and traditions.

The church was established in the late fifteenth century, after Málaga came under the rule of the Catholic Monarchs. Like many buildings in cities with long Islamic histories, it did not emerge in a cultural vacuum. Builders and patrons worked within an existing urban environment, using familiar techniques and adapting architectural forms. The tower is especially notable for its appearance, which is often discussed in relation to the transition from Islamic architectural traditions to Christian church design. It offers a reminder that changes in political and religious authority do not instantly erase earlier ways of building.

The church is also associated with Pablo Picasso. He was baptised here in 1881, the year of his birth in Málaga. That connection brings the building into the story of the artist’s childhood. Picasso’s later life and work became international, but the early stages of his biography were rooted in the city’s streets, family networks and institutions. A baptismal record is a different kind of historical evidence from a painting, yet it can establish a concrete connection between a famous person and a particular place.

As you look at the façade, notice the contrast between the more restrained surfaces and the tower’s distinctive form. The building has been altered and embellished over the centuries, so it is better understood as a layered structure than as a perfectly preserved example of one style. Architectural history often works this way: a church remains in use, and each generation repairs, changes or reinterprets the building it inherits.

The church’s religious function matters too. Historic churches were not merely decorative landmarks. They served local communities through worship, ceremonies and major moments in family life. Their position in the city also made them familiar points of reference, shaping routes and neighbourhood identity.

Iglesia de Santiago connects several strands of Málaga’s history: the establishment of Christian institutions after 1487, the persistence and transformation of architectural traditions, and Picasso’s early life. It is worth looking at it not just as an old church, but as a place that has continued to gather meaning as the city around it changed.
