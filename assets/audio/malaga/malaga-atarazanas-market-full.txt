Atarazanas Market is one of Málaga’s most active historic buildings, combining a nineteenth-century market hall with a gateway that dates back to the city’s Nasrid period. The name *atarazanas* comes from a word associated with shipyards, and the area once contained facilities for shipbuilding when Málaga’s waterfront lay closer to this part of the city.

The most striking historical feature is the old marble entrance, which survived after the original shipyard complex disappeared. When the city developed a new municipal market in the nineteenth century, architect Joaquín de Rucoba designed a building that incorporated the medieval gateway into its main façade. The market opened in 1879. Rather than removing the older structure, the project made it the visual centre of a new building.

Look at the contrast between the stone portal and the metal framework behind it. The market’s structure reflects nineteenth-century engineering, when iron made it possible to create large, open interiors suited to public commerce. Its decorative style also drew on Islamic and Moorish architectural forms, which were frequently reinterpreted in Spanish architecture during this period. The result is not a medieval market preserved unchanged, but a modern market building that deliberately carries a fragment of the city’s earlier history.

Inside, the market is organised around the practical needs of buying and selling food. Stalls display fish and seafood, meat, fruit, vegetables and other produce. Málaga’s position on the coast and its connections with the fertile countryside of Andalusia are reflected in the range of goods available. The market is a place where local food traditions meet the daily routines of residents and the curiosity of visitors.

Markets have long been important social spaces. They are places to compare prices, exchange news, ask for advice and maintain relationships between sellers and customers. Their importance cannot be measured only by the age of the building. The activity taking place inside gives the market its continuing role in the city.

Atarazanas was declared a protected cultural property, and restoration work in the early twenty-first century sought to recover the building’s architectural character. Conservation in a working market requires balancing the needs of vendors and shoppers with the preservation of historic fabric. The building’s continuing use helps keep it connected to everyday life rather than turning it into a static monument.

As you look at the entrance, imagine the different roles this site has played. A medieval shipyard served maritime needs; a nineteenth-century market supported a growing city; today, the market continues to bring food and people together. The gateway links those periods physically, while the market’s daily activity keeps the story alive.
