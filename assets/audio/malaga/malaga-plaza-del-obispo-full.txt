Plaza del Obispo offers one of the clearest views of Málaga Cathedral. Stand back far enough to see the façade as a whole, then notice how the square frames the building and guides your attention toward it.

The square takes its name from the bishop’s palace, which stands alongside the cathedral. Together, the palace and cathedral mark the importance of the church in Málaga’s historic centre. In many European cities, bishops’ residences, cathedrals and open squares formed a connected civic landscape. They were religious spaces, but they also occupied prominent positions in the daily life and visual identity of the city.

Málaga’s cathedral was established after Christian forces captured the city in 1487, on the site of the former main mosque. The present building took shape from the sixteenth century onward and continued to develop over several generations. Renaissance planning and later Baroque decoration give the cathedral a layered appearance rather than the unified look of a building completed in a single period.

The missing second tower is one of its most distinctive features. The local nickname La Manquita means “the one-armed lady”. Popular stories often offer a simple explanation for the unfinished tower, but the building’s history involved changes in funding and priorities over a long period. The absence is a visible reminder that major monuments are shaped by practical decisions as well as artistic plans.

The square itself creates a useful viewing distance. From close beside the cathedral, it can be difficult to understand the whole façade; from here, the building’s vertical emphasis and decorative details become easier to read. The paving, surrounding buildings and open space all help establish the cathedral as the dominant feature without separating it from the surrounding city.

This is a place to notice the relationship between architecture and movement. People cross the square on their way through the centre, stop for photographs or pause at nearby cafés. The cathedral remains a working religious building, but it is also part of Málaga’s public image and everyday geography.

Look from the palace to the cathedral and back across the square. The two buildings speak to the institutional history of the city, while the square gives that history a public setting. It is a small space, but it helps explain how Málaga’s historic centre was organised around landmarks that were meant to be seen, approached and used.
