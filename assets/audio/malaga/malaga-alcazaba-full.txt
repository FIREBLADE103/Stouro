The Alcazaba rises above Málaga’s historic centre, its walls following the lower slopes of Mount Gibralfaro. The name comes from Arabic *al-qasba*, meaning an urban fortress, but the site’s history reaches back much further than the surviving Islamic structures.

Phoenician settlers established a presence in the area in antiquity, and the Romans also occupied the slopes around the hill. Their Roman Theatre survives just below the Alcazaba. During the Islamic period, the fortress developed from a defensive stronghold into a palace-fortress and centre of government. The site’s position overlooking the city and bay made it valuable both militarily and politically.

The Alcazaba was built and altered over several centuries. Its walls, gates and towers follow the terrain, creating a layered system of defence rather than a single simple enclosure. The approach through gates and passages could slow movement and make the route easier to defend. Inside, however, the character changes. Courtyards, gardens, water features and residential spaces reveal that this was also a place of rule and daily life.

Water was especially important in the climate of southern Spain. Gardens and courtyards could provide shade and a calmer environment within the fortified complex, while channels and storage systems helped manage a vital resource. These details remind us that a palace-fortress had to be practical as well as impressive. It needed to house people, support administration and maintain a household, not merely withstand an attack.

The Alcazaba’s relationship with Gibralfaro is central to understanding the site. The hilltop castle above was developed as a stronger defensive position and connected to the Alcazaba by the fortified Coracha. Together, they formed a linked system that controlled the hillside and protected the city. From below, the two fortifications can look like one continuous complex, but they had distinct roles.

Málaga fell to the Catholic Monarchs in 1487 after a long siege. The conquest changed the political and religious order of the city, and the Alcazaba’s use and condition evolved under Christian rule. Over time, parts of the complex deteriorated and were adapted for new purposes. Restoration in the modern period has made much of the surviving architecture accessible again, though visitors see a structure shaped by repair and reconstruction as well as medieval building.

Look back toward the Roman Theatre below the walls. The proximity of the two sites makes the city’s layered history unusually visible. Roman public entertainment, Islamic government and later Christian rule all left traces on the same hillside. The Alcazaba is therefore more than a picturesque fortress. It is evidence of how Málaga’s strategic location attracted different societies, each of which reused the landscape and built upon what came before.
