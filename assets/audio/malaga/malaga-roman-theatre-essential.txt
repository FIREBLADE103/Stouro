Look at the stone seating and curved outline below the Alcazaba. This is Málaga’s Roman Theatre, built in the early years of the first century CE, during the age of the emperor Augustus.

Like theatres throughout the Roman world, it was designed to bring a large audience together for performances. The seating rises in a curve, allowing people to see the stage from different levels. The builders used the natural slope of the hill for part of the structure and created additional support where needed.

The theatre remained in use for centuries, before eventually falling out of use. Some of its stonework was later reused in buildings on the hillside, including the Alcazaba. That reuse was practical: cut stone was valuable building material, and older structures often became quarries for newer ones.

The theatre was rediscovered in 1951 during work in this part of the city. For centuries it had been hidden beneath later buildings and streets. Archaeological investigation and restoration gradually made the remains visible again.

Here, Málaga’s history is layered almost literally. Roman entertainment, Islamic fortification and modern urban life occupy the same hillside. The theatre is not just a ruin; it is evidence of how one city has repeatedly built over, reused and rediscovered its past.
