Look up the hillside at the Alcazaba’s walls and towers. Its name comes from the Arabic *al-qasba*, meaning an urban fortress. It began as a defensive stronghold and developed into a palace-fortress and seat of government during Málaga’s Islamic period.

The site had a much longer history before that. Phoenician and Roman activity took place around the hill, and the Roman Theatre survives just below the fortress. The Alcazaba as we know it developed over time, with important building phases during the eleventh century and later periods.

Its designers made use of the slope, linking defensive walls with courtyards, gates and inner spaces. The result was not simply a castle built for battle; it was also a place where rulers lived and governed. The contrast between outer defences and more intimate interior spaces is one of its defining features.

Málaga was captured by the Catholic Monarchs in 1487, after a long siege. The Alcazaba then changed function and condition over the following centuries, before restoration work helped preserve it as a major historic monument.

From the street, the walls can look like a single fortress. Inside, the Alcazaba reveals a more complex world of defence, administration, water and domestic life.
