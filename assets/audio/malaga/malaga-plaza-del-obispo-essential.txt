Look across this small square toward the main façade of Málaga Cathedral. Plaza del Obispo is one of the best places to appreciate the relationship between the cathedral and the historic centre.

The square takes its name from the bishop’s palace, which stands beside the cathedral. Its architecture and open space create a formal approach to one of Málaga’s most important religious buildings. The square is relatively compact, so the cathedral’s scale becomes especially noticeable when you stand here.

The present cathedral developed over several centuries after the Christian conquest of Málaga in 1487. Its long construction history helps explain the mixture of Renaissance and Baroque features visible around the city. The unfinished second tower gave it the popular nickname La Manquita, “the one-armed lady”.

This square also shows how religious architecture shaped the city’s public spaces. The cathedral is not isolated behind walls; its façade opens onto a place where people gather, pass through and meet.

Take a moment to compare the proportions of the square with the height of the building. The space gives the cathedral room to be seen, while the cathedral gives the square its identity. Here, architecture and public life have been linked for centuries.
