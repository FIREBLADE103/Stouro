Look at the grand entrance to Atarazanas Market. The market’s name recalls the Arabic word for a shipyard, because this area once housed Nasrid-era shipbuilding facilities near the waterfront.

The surviving marble entrance is a remnant of that earlier complex. It was incorporated into the market building when architect Joaquín de Rucoba designed a new municipal market in the nineteenth century. The market opened in 1879, combining a historic gateway with an iron structure and a style influenced by Moorish architecture.

Inside, the market is a place of daily commerce rather than a museum. Stalls sell fish, meat, fruit, vegetables and other produce, reflecting Málaga’s position between the Mediterranean coast and the agricultural lands of Andalusia.

Look at how the old stone gateway contrasts with the lighter metal structure of the market. The building brings together different periods and purposes: medieval maritime industry, nineteenth-century urban planning and today’s food culture.

Markets are useful places to understand a city through its routines. People come here to shop, talk, eat and meet. Atarazanas is historic, but its story continues every day through the trade that takes place inside.
