The Church of the Sacred Heart stands out in central Málaga for its neo-Gothic design. Its pointed arches, vertical emphasis and elaborate stone details recall medieval Gothic churches, even though the building belongs to a much later period.

The church was built in the early twentieth century for the Jesuits and designed by Fernando Guerrero-Strachan, an architect associated with Málaga’s changing urban landscape. Neo-Gothic architecture became popular across Europe during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Architects used medieval forms to express religious devotion, continuity and a sense of historical rootedness at a time when cities were changing rapidly.

Look at the way the façade draws the eye upward. Pointed arches and tall narrow openings create a feeling of height, while decorative details give the surface texture and complexity. These are familiar Gothic signals, but the building is not a medieval survival. It is a modern interpretation of a historic style, selected because of the meanings that style carried.

That distinction matters. Architectural styles are often revived because they seem to express values that a patron or community wants to emphasise. Gothic architecture had long been associated with major Christian churches, and its revival allowed a new building to evoke the authority and spirituality of that tradition. The result was not a neutral copy of the Middle Ages but a statement made in a modern city.

Málaga is especially interesting in this respect because its historic centre contains buildings from many periods. Islamic fortifications and palaces, Renaissance churches, Baroque façades and nineteenth-century commercial streets all occupy the same urban landscape. The Church of the Sacred Heart adds another chapter: a twentieth-century building looking back to medieval forms.

Its position in the street also shapes how it is experienced. Rather than approaching across a vast open square, visitors often encounter the church within the surrounding urban fabric. The façade rises above the street and rewards a closer look at its architectural detail.

The church remains a religious space, so its meaning is not only architectural. For worshippers, the building continues to serve a living community. For visitors, it offers an example of how historic styles can be reinterpreted to meet the needs and ambitions of a later generation.

As you look at the pointed arches and towers, remember that architectural history is not a straight line in which one style replaces another and disappears. Older forms can return, carrying new meanings. This church shows Málaga continuing to build its identity through a dialogue with the past.
