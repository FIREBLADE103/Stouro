Imagine arriving here on an ordinary day in ancient Rome. Around you, people are moving between shops, temples, public offices and gathering places. Someone is conducting business; elsewhere, a religious ceremony is under way. A speaker may be addressing a crowd, while officials and citizens move through the same busy public space.

This was the Roman Forum, the centre of civic life for more than a thousand years.

The valley was originally marshy ground. During Rome's early development, drainage and other engineering works made it possible to build here. By the late seventh century BC, the Forum was beginning to take shape. Its position between the Palatine and Capitoline hills made it a natural meeting point as the city expanded.

The Forum was never a single, finished design. It grew in stages as Rome's needs and ambitions changed. Early spaces for trade and religious activity were joined by buildings for political business and public administration. Later, grand basilicas provided space for legal proceedings and commercial dealings. Temples, monuments and paved routes gave the area a monumental character, but its purpose remained practical as well as symbolic.

The Forum's religious and political roles were closely connected. Romans gathered here to honour their gods, observe rituals and mark important public occasions. At the same time, this was a setting for speeches, elections and the business of government. Political life could be intensely personal and competitive, and public spaces such as this gave leaders an audience.

Follow the line of the Via Sacra, the Sacred Way, which passed through the Forum and formed part of important religious and triumphal processions. Victorious commanders and emperors could use monuments and ceremonies here to present military success as a benefit to Rome. The buildings were not merely decoration: they helped tell the story that Roman power wanted to tell about itself.

Look towards the Arch of Septimius Severus, erected in AD 203 to celebrate victories in the east. Its carved reliefs and inscriptions made military achievement visible in the very heart of the capital. Nearby monuments honoured emperors and gods, linking the city's present to its traditions and its rulers.

As the empire grew, the Forum became crowded with monuments. Emperors added prestigious buildings, sometimes replacing or transforming earlier structures. The area continued to serve public functions, but Rome's political and administrative life became more complex, and new imperial forums were built nearby.

After the ancient city's institutions changed, the Forum lost its former central role. Buildings were damaged, dismantled or reused, and the ground level rose as debris and soil accumulated. Parts of the ancient landscape disappeared beneath later development.

The archaeological site you see today is therefore a fragment of a much larger story. Columns and foundations survive, but the noise, movement and daily routines that once filled the Forum are gone.

Try to imagine those ruins as a living city centre. The Forum was where religion, business, law, politics and public spectacle overlapped. To understand Rome, you need to understand not just its emperors and armies, but the public spaces where the city continually negotiated what it meant to be Roman.
