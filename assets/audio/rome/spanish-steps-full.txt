The Spanish Steps are one of Rome's most famous gathering places, but their story begins with a practical challenge: how to connect the lower Piazza di Spagna with the church of Trinità dei Monti on the hill above.

Before the staircase was built, the steep slope between these two points was awkward to cross. The project was discussed for years before the architect Francesco De Sanctis designed the elegant solution that survives today. Construction took place between 1723 and 1726, and the staircase was inaugurated during the Jubilee of 1725.

The name can be confusing. The steps are called Spanish because of the Spanish presence and diplomatic connections around Piazza di Spagna below. The church at the top, however, has strong French associations. The staircase therefore became a link between two areas shaped by different European powers and communities.

Rather than creating a single straight flight, De Sanctis arranged the steps in a sequence of ramps, landings and curves. The changing directions soften the climb and create a sense of movement. As you look up, the staircase seems to unfold gradually, drawing the eye towards the church. From above, the broad steps open out towards the piazza and the streets of the historic centre.

The design reflects the eighteenth-century taste for theatrical urban spaces. Architecture was not limited to palaces and churches; it could also shape the experience of moving through a city. The steps turn an ordinary journey uphill into a public setting where people can pause, meet and look out over Rome.

At the top stands Trinità dei Monti, a church founded in connection with the French community in Rome. Its position gives the staircase a clear focal point, while the surrounding buildings frame the view. At the bottom, Piazza di Spagna became a busy part of the city, with streets leading towards shops, cafés and other landmarks.

Over the centuries, the steps have appeared in paintings, photographs, films and travel writing. Their fame comes partly from their beauty, but also from the simple human activities they accommodate. Visitors climb them, meet friends nearby and stop to admire the view. The staircase feels monumental without being detached from everyday life.

Spring brings a particularly colourful tradition. From around mid-April to mid-May, azaleas are displayed near the upper part of the steps, creating a striking contrast with the pale stone. The seasonal display has become a familiar part of Rome's arrival into warmer weather.

The Spanish Steps have also needed careful restoration to protect the stone and preserve their appearance. Their popularity makes conservation important, especially in a city where historic monuments remain part of daily public life. Visitors should follow current rules and signage rather than treating the steps as seating or a picnic area.

As you take in the staircase, notice how it connects several layers of the city: the piazza below, the church above, and the eighteenth-century vision that brought them together. Unlike the ancient ruins elsewhere on this tour, the Spanish Steps belong to a later Rome — a city of diplomatic neighbourhoods, grand urban design and public spaces made beautiful through careful architecture.

Their success lies in making a practical connection feel like an experience in its own right. The climb, the changing views and the movement of people all contribute to the character of this corner of Rome.
