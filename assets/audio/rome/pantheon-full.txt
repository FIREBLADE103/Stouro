Take a moment to study the Pantheon's entrance. The portico, with its towering granite columns, looks like the gateway to an ancient temple — and that is exactly the impression its designers intended.

The inscription honours Marcus Agrippa, a close ally and son-in-law of Augustus, Rome's first emperor. Agrippa commissioned an earlier building on this site in the first century BC. However, the structure standing here today dates mainly from the reign of Emperor Hadrian, who rebuilt the Pantheon in the second century AD. The old inscription was retained, connecting the new monument to Rome's earlier history.

The name Pantheon is commonly understood to refer to all the gods, although the precise original religious meaning remains debated. Whatever the exact intention, the building's scale and unusual design made it one of the most ambitious architectural spaces in the Roman world.

The real surprise comes inside. The circular interior is covered by a vast concrete dome, approximately 43.3 metres across. Its diameter is almost exactly equal to the height from the floor to the top of the dome. Imagine a perfect sphere fitting inside the space: that gives a sense of the proportions its builders achieved.

Look up to the centre, where a circular opening called the oculus exposes the interior to the sky. It is the dome's only source of direct natural light. As the sun moves, the light shifts across the walls and floor, changing the atmosphere throughout the day. Rain can enter through the opening, and the slightly sloping floor directs water towards drainage holes.

How did Roman builders create a dome of this size? They used a combination of structural ingenuity and carefully selected materials. The supporting walls are enormously thick, and the dome becomes lighter towards the top. Its concrete incorporates different aggregates at different levels, reducing weight where it matters most. The recessed square panels, known as coffers, also reduce the mass while creating the distinctive geometric pattern overhead.

The building's later history is just as important as its engineering. In AD 609, the Pantheon was consecrated as a Christian church, dedicated to St Mary and the Martyrs. That change of use helped preserve it through centuries in which other ancient structures were abandoned, damaged or dismantled for their materials.

The Pantheon also became a place of burial. Among those interred here are the Renaissance artist Raphael and several Italian kings. This continued use gave the ancient building new meanings, allowing it to remain part of Rome's living religious and civic landscape.

Even the façade tells a story of survival and change. The inscription refers to Agrippa, while the monumental form behind it belongs to Hadrian's rebuilding. The building therefore connects different periods of Roman history in a single view.

Whether you admire the Pantheon from the piazza or stand beneath its dome, look beyond its age. It is a demonstration of how Roman architects combined proportion, materials and engineering to shape an extraordinary interior — and how a building can survive by acquiring new purposes across the centuries.
