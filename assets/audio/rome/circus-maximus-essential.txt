Look across this long valley between the Palatine and Aventine hills. Today it is an open space, but in ancient Rome it was the Circus Maximus, the city's great chariot-racing venue.

At roughly 600 metres long, it was enormous. Generations of Romans gathered here to watch teams of charioteers race around a central barrier, with the crowd cheering for their favourite colours.

These races were more than sport. Successful drivers could become celebrities, and rival supporters brought intense loyalty to the stands. The Circus also hosted public ceremonies, processions and celebrations of military victories.

The venue developed over centuries. Julius Caesar added substantial permanent structures, and Emperor Trajan later carried out a major rebuilding after fires had damaged it.

Very little of the grand seating survives above ground today, so use the shape of the valley to imagine the scale. The track once filled this space, surrounded by tiers of spectators.

The Circus Maximus shows another side of Roman life: a city that expressed its power not only through temples and government, but also through huge shared spectacles.
