 Imagine approaching this building as a spectator in ancient Rome. Around you, people are arriving for a day of public entertainment. Beyond these walls, the city is busy; inside, the arena is being prepared for the day's events.

Construction began under Emperor Vespasian and the amphitheatre opened under his son Titus in the year eighty. Its ancient name was the Flavian Amphitheatre, after the dynasty responsible for its construction. The name we use today probably comes from a colossal statue that stood nearby, rather than simply from the building's enormous size.

Now look at the repeated arches. They are not just decoration. They formed part of a carefully planned circulation system, helping spectators enter, find their sections and leave. Admission was free, according to the archaeological park, but that did not mean everyone was treated equally. Seating followed a strict hierarchy, with the best positions reserved for the emperor and senior social groups.

The arena hosted gladiatorial contests and hunts involving wild animals. These events were carefully organised spectacles, supported by workers and elaborate equipment that most spectators could not see.

Beneath the arena floor, hidden corridors and machinery made sudden appearances possible. Trapdoors and lifting platforms brought people, animals and props into the arena. The underground arrangements developed over time: the initial systems were built in wood, and later emperors introduced substantial masonry structures and altered the machinery.

The building's history did not end when its role as an entertainment venue declined. It went through centuries of changing uses, damage and repair. Earthquakes contributed to its destruction, and valuable building materials were removed and reused elsewhere. Archaeologists can still study traces left behind by the blocks that were taken away.

So when you look at the surviving façade, remember that you are seeing more than the remains of an arena. You are looking at a building that organised enormous crowds, staged complex public spectacles and reflected the inequalities of Roman society.

Its original shows are long gone, but the structure still reveals the ambition, ingenuity and power that shaped ancient Rome.
