Imagine standing on this hill before Rome became an empire. The settlement below you is not yet the vast capital described by ancient writers. Instead, this is a landscape of small communities, developing over generations into one of the most influential cities in history.

The Palatine preserves evidence of some of Rome's earliest settlement. Archaeologists have found traces of dwellings dating back to the Iron Age, long before the emperors whose names are associated with the hill today.

Roman tradition gave the place an even older significance. According to legend, Romulus founded Rome here in 753 BC after a conflict with his twin brother Remus. The story helped Romans explain their origins and gave the Palatine a special place in their identity. The legend is not the same as archaeological proof, but it shows how closely the hill was tied to Rome's idea of itself.

As the city grew, the Palatine became a desirable address for the wealthy. During the late Republic, prominent Romans built substantial homes here, decorated with painted walls, mosaics and carefully finished floors. The remains of houses such as the House of the Griffins give archaeologists a glimpse of the luxurious domestic world that existed before the emperors.

Then Augustus changed the hill's role. Rome's first emperor made his residence here, establishing a powerful association between the Palatine and the imperial household. His choice was significant: he ruled a city that still valued its republican traditions, yet his home stood above the centre of Roman public life.

Later emperors expanded the complex dramatically. New palaces, reception rooms, courtyards and gardens transformed much of the hill. These were not simply private homes. They were settings for government, ceremony and the display of imperial status. The emperor's residence became a world of its own, separated from ordinary life while remaining close to the Forum and the heart of the city.

Over time, the word “Palatine” became linked with royal and elite residences. The modern word “palace” ultimately reflects that association.

Today, the surviving walls can be difficult to picture as complete buildings. Try to imagine them with painted surfaces, marble decoration, colonnades and gardens. The ruins are only the framework of a much richer environment.

From this hill, the story of Rome becomes easier to understand. The Palatine links the city's legendary foundation with its earliest settlements, its wealthy Republican families and the emperors who reshaped the capital. Few places reveal so clearly how Rome turned its past into a source of political power.
