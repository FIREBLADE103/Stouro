The Trevi Fountain is one of Rome's most recognisable sights, but its dramatic appearance hides a story that stretches from ancient engineering to the ambitions of eighteenth-century popes.

The fountain stands against the Palazzo Poli, turning the rear of the palace into a theatrical stone landscape. Its construction began in 1732, after Pope Clement XII organised a competition to choose a design. The winning proposal came from Nicola Salvi, an architect who combined monumental sculpture with the movement and sound of flowing water. Salvi died before the project was finished, and Giuseppe Pannini completed the work in 1762.

At the centre is Oceanus, a figure representing the great waters. He rides in a shell-shaped chariot drawn by two horses, guided by tritons. One horse is restless while the other is calm, suggesting that water can be both turbulent and controlled. Around them, the sculpted rockwork creates the impression that the figures are emerging from a natural landscape, even though the entire scene is carefully composed architecture.

The fountain is more than a decorative masterpiece. It marks the endpoint of the Aqua Virgo, an aqueduct built in 19 BC under Marcus Agrippa's water programme. The aqueduct brought water into Rome from springs outside the city and has continued to supply fountains through a remarkable history of repair and restoration. Its modern name, Acqua Vergine, preserves the ancient connection.

The aqueduct's traditional story tells of a young girl who showed Roman soldiers where to find the spring. That legend appears in the fountain's sculptural programme, alongside a relief referring to Agrippa and the construction of the water system. As with many ancient stories, the legend should be understood as tradition rather than a fully documented account.

Look at the fountain as a whole. The architecture resembles a triumphal arch, while the sculptures celebrate the benefits and power of water. The combination is characteristic of Baroque Rome, where art and public monuments often expressed the authority of the papacy while improving or beautifying the city.

The Trevi Fountain has also become part of popular culture. Its appearance in Federico Fellini's film *La Dolce Vita* made it famous to an even wider international audience, although the scene is a cinematic moment rather than a guide to how visitors should behave at the monument.

Then there is the coin tradition. Visitors commonly throw a coin over one shoulder into the water, following the belief that doing so will bring them back to Rome. It is a modern custom with variations in how people describe it, but its meaning is easy to understand: a wish to return to a city that has made a lasting impression.

The fountain has required restoration over time, including major work in the late twentieth century and a more recent restoration supported by the fashion house Fendi. These efforts help protect the stonework and keep the water system working under the pressure of enormous visitor numbers.

Today, the Trevi Fountain remains a meeting point of engineering, art, religion, legend and tourism. Its water still arrives through a system whose origins lie in ancient Rome, while its sculpted façade reflects the ambition of a much later age. When you look at it, you are seeing not just a fountain, but centuries of Rome's relationship with water — and with the power of public spectacle.
