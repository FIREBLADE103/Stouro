Capitoline Hill is one of the smallest of Rome's famous hills, but its importance in the city's history is enormous. From ancient times, this was a place of religion, ceremony and public authority.

Archaeological discoveries show that people lived on and around the hill long before Rome became a Mediterranean power. Later Roman tradition surrounded the Capitoline with stories about the city's origins and its relationship with the gods. These traditions helped make the hill a symbolic centre of Roman identity.

At the summit once stood the Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, dedicated to Jupiter, Juno and Minerva — the three deities known as the Capitoline Triad. The temple was traditionally associated with Rome's early kings and was inaugurated in 509 BC, around the beginning of the Republic. It was an imposing structure, with a high podium and a broad, columned façade.

For Romans, the temple was far more than a place of worship. Jupiter was associated with the protection and authority of the state, and the sanctuary became a setting for major public ceremonies. Victorious commanders could celebrate their achievements in ways that connected military success with Rome's gods. The message was clear: Rome's power was not merely the result of human effort, but part of a larger, divinely favoured order.

The Capitoline also overlooked the political heart of the city. From here, the Forum lay below, with its temples, public buildings and gathering places. The hill's religious significance and its position beside the Forum made it a natural symbol of Roman authority.

The ancient temples were damaged and rebuilt over the centuries, and little of the great Temple of Jupiter survives above ground today. Yet the Capitoline's role did not disappear with the decline of ancient Roman religion. During the Middle Ages, the hill developed into a centre of civic government. The Palazzo Senatorio became associated with the municipal administration, and it remains Rome's city hall today.

The square you see now reflects another important chapter. Piazza del Campidoglio was redesigned in the Renaissance, with a plan associated with Michelangelo. Its geometry, façades and central statue create a unified architectural space, very different from the irregular streets and monumental ruins of the ancient city. The buildings around the square also house the Capitoline Museums, whose collections include works that tell the story of Rome through sculpture, inscriptions and other objects recovered from the city and its surroundings.

This continuity is what makes the Capitoline so distinctive. The buildings and institutions have changed, but the hill has repeatedly been used to express the identity and authority of Rome. In antiquity, that authority was closely tied to the gods and the state. In later centuries, it became associated with civic government and the city's cultural heritage.

As you look across the surrounding rooftops and down towards the Forum, imagine the different generations who have stood here: priests, generals, senators, medieval officials and Renaissance artists. Each inherited a place already rich with meaning, and each added something to its story.

The Capitoline Hill is not simply a viewpoint over ancient Rome. It is a reminder that the city's history is not confined to the ruins below. Rome has repeatedly rebuilt its institutions and its identity while continuing to draw on the places that made it important in the first place.
