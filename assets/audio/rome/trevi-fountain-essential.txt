Listen to the water and look at the scale of the Trevi Fountain. Set against the Palazzo Poli, it feels almost like the façade of a palace opening into a rocky landscape.

The fountain was designed by Nicola Salvi after Pope Clement XII launched a competition in 1732. It was completed after Salvi's death by Giuseppe Pannini.

At its centre stands Oceanus, the personification of the great waters, riding in a shell-shaped chariot pulled by two horses. One horse appears restless, the other calm, reflecting the changing nature of the sea.

But the fountain's story reaches further back. Its water comes from the Aqua Virgo, an ancient aqueduct traditionally dated to 19 BC, which still supplies several fountains in Rome.

And then there is the custom of tossing a coin over your shoulder into the water. Tradition says it means you will return to Rome. It is a small gesture, but one that has become part of the city's modern identity.

The Trevi Fountain combines ancient engineering, Baroque sculpture and a simple wish to come back to the Eternal City.
