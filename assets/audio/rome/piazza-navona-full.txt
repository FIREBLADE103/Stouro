Piazza Navona is one of Rome's most beautiful public spaces, but its distinctive shape is not simply the result of an architect's imagination. The square follows the outline of an ancient stadium, meaning that the layout of a modern piazza still reflects the entertainment culture of imperial Rome.

In AD 86, Emperor Domitian built a stadium here for athletic contests. These competitions were influenced by Greek traditions and differed from the chariot races held at the Circus Maximus. The long track, with its curved end, was designed for events in which athletes competed before large crowds. The stadium could accommodate many thousands of spectators, and its footprint remains visible in the shape of the square today.

As the ancient buildings fell out of use, houses and other structures gradually occupied the site. The stadium's outline survived beneath them, giving the area its unusually long form. The name Navona is generally linked to the Latin word *agones*, associated with contests and games.

The square took on its present Baroque character particularly during the seventeenth century. Under Pope Innocent X, whose family palace faces the piazza, the area was transformed into a setting for grand architecture and papal display. The result was a dramatic public space framed by façades, fountains and the church of Sant'Agnese in Agone.

At the centre stands Bernini's Fountain of the Four Rivers, completed in 1651. A rugged rock formation supports an obelisk, while four large sculpted figures represent the Nile, Ganges, Danube and Río de la Plata. Together, these rivers symbolised the continents recognised by Europeans at the time: Africa, Asia, Europe and the Americas. Their poses and surrounding details give each figure its own character.

The Nile's veiled head is traditionally understood as a reference to the river's then-unknown source. The Ganges carries an oar, the Danube is associated with a horse, and the Río de la Plata appears with an arm raised. The composition combines geography, artistic imagination and a message about the reach of papal authority.

Two other fountains complete the square's arrangement. The Fountain of the Moor stands at the southern end, while the Fountain of Neptune is at the northern end. Together, they make water and sculpture central to the piazza's identity.

Across the square, Sant'Agnese in Agone contributes another layer of Baroque drama. The church is associated with architects including the Rainaldi family and Francesco Borromini, whose work shaped its distinctive façade. The surrounding buildings make the square feel like a carefully composed outdoor theatre.

Piazza Navona was also a place for public amusement. For a period, especially on summer weekends in August, the square was flooded to create a shallow expanse of water for entertainment and relief from the heat. This practice continued into the nineteenth century.

Today, the piazza is full of cafés, artists, visitors and everyday Roman life. Yet its outline still preserves the ancient stadium, while its fountains and façades tell the story of a later age that reshaped the city through art and architecture.

As you walk along the square, imagine the layers beneath your feet: athletes competing in the stadium, medieval homes filling the old footprint, and Baroque patrons transforming the space into a grand urban stage. Piazza Navona is a striking example of Rome's ability to give an ancient place a new life without entirely erasing its past.
