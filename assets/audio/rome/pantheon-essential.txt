Look at the Pantheon's entrance. Those enormous granite columns lead into one of the best-preserved buildings of ancient Rome.

The inscription above them names Marcus Agrippa, who commissioned an earlier temple here in the first century BC. But the building you see today was reconstructed under Emperor Hadrian in the second century AD.

Step inside, if you have the opportunity, and look upwards. The vast concrete dome is as wide as the interior is high, creating a remarkable sense of balance. At its centre is the oculus, a circular opening that lets daylight — and rain — enter the building.

The dome's weight is carefully managed through the structure's design and the use of lighter materials towards the top. Roman builders achieved something extraordinary without modern steel reinforcement.

The Pantheon survived while many ancient buildings disappeared, partly because it was converted into a Christian church in AD 609.

Its name, engineering and long history make it one of Rome's most remarkable links between the ancient world and the present.
